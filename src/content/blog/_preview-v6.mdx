<Callout type="info" label="TL;DR">
When a voice agent has to capture a critical value — an ID, a medication, a street address — over a noisy line, how does it know it heard right? The natural answer is the speech-to-text (ASR) model's own **confidence score**. But that score measures how confidently the model decoded *some* transcript, not whether the transcript is *correct* — and it's least reliable on the degraded audio where you need it most. Scaling the model up doesn't rescue you: counterintuitively, the bigger model is *more* likely to commit a wrong answer, because it stops abstaining and its confidence floats up past a threshold tuned for the smaller one.

I built a benchmark for one high-stakes failure mode — reliable ID capture over degraded audio — then upgraded the ASR model in my agent. The pipeline got *less* safe: on deliberately-unrecoverable audio, wrong commits rose from **0% to 64%**. This post is how I caught that regression and what's underneath it — what the confidence number actually measures, and why bigger models on worse audio stop knowing when to keep quiet.
</Callout>


## The question

Voice agents are being deployed in increasingly high-stakes scenarios. We're still in the early phases of the rollout. Today, a critical failure often means lost revenues. But a future where we rely on voice agents for safety-critical roles means that effective evaluation becomes essential for health and safety.

Imagine a medical scribe agent capturing the wrong medication name, an emergency dispatch agent capturing the wrong street address, or an air traffic control agent hearing the wrong flight number. These errors could result in harm to humans.

The examples above are all instances of the critical failure mode of mishearing key information. The thing the agent is supposed to do when it can't reliably hear is *escalate to a human*. So the practical question, for anyone building one of these agents, is: how does it know?

## The obvious answer, and why it isn't enough

Most ASR models expose some kind of confidence number. Whisper, for example, kindly provides you the per-token probabilities of the transcript it decoded. A decent way to represent its overall confidence in its transcription then is `exp(avg_logprob)`, the geometric mean of the per-token probabilities.[^2] You can then wire that into an escalation policy: above some threshold, commit; below another, escalate; in between, ask the rep to repeat.

The catch is that this number is measuring the wrong thing. Read the definition carefully: it scores how confidently Whisper produced *the tokens it chose*, not whether those tokens are *true*. It is confidence about the words, not about the world. On clean audio those two things travel together, so it's easy to mistake one for the other. On degraded audio they come apart — and the gap between them is exactly where a high-stakes agent gets hurt.

The surprising part in my benchmark was that this failure got *worse* as I upgraded Whisper. Larger models captured more recoverable IDs, as expected. But on the deliberately-unrecoverable audio, they also produced more confident wrong transcripts that sailed through the commit gate. The result was a model upgrade that looked better on capture rate while becoming less safe on exactly the cases where the right answer was "I can't hear this."

I want to be precise about the claim, because there's a lazy version and a real one. The lazy version is "bigger Whisper hallucinates more." The real version, which the data below actually supports, is two-fold: bigger models **stop abstaining** on unrecoverable audio — they lose the one move that fails safe — *and* their whole confidence scale **shifts upward**, so a threshold tuned on a smaller model lets more of those non-abstained wrong answers through. A single confidence threshold is supposed to guard against exactly this, and the second half of this post is about why it can't.

## The benchmark

<AgentFlowDemo />

The benchmark simulates the challenge of capturing essential info over degraded audio channels. I generated 100 test cases (200 audio fixtures total, two attempts per case) using the ElevenLabs TTS API. Each fixture is a short spoken identifier, e.g. *"S as in sierra, X-ray, one, one, eight, five"* (so the canonical ground truth here would be: `SX1185`). The cases span five buckets of increasingly degraded audio. Fixtures in the 'severe' bucket are designed to be unrecoverable.

| Bucket | n | Distortion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| clean | 15 | none |
| light | 15 | mild noise OR phone bandpass alone |
| moderate | 20 | phone bandpass + 10 dB SNR noise |
| heavy | 25 | phone bandpass + 5 dB SNR + light dropout |
| severe | 25 | phone bandpass + −12 dB SNR + 250 ms dropout @ 55% per-window |

### Simulating phone distortion
The deterministic degradation pipeline simulates the sort of distortion artifacts that you hear on a phone call (you might say it's a bit phone-y[^1]): a 300–3400 Hz bandpass (the standard phone band), a μ-law codec roundtrip (the standard phone codec), additive white noise at a configurable SNR, and packet dropout where 250 ms windows are silenced at a configurable probability. The severe bucket layers all four and pushes things beyond human-level recoverability. Also, the catalog is seeded, so rerunning the generator produces byte-identical fixtures.

### The agent loop
For each case, the agent being tested receives the audio file and chooses one of three actions: commit a transcription, ask the rep to repeat (one retry allowed), or escalate to a human. To recap, on the cases where the audio is actually unintelligible, the right behavior is to escalate. If your agent is recording something like a medication, an emergency address, or a patient identifier, you want it to fail loudly. The benchmark then grades the agent on % accuracy as well as escalation precision/recall (i.e. to what extent did it escalate on impossible cases but not on tractable cases?).

To exercise the benchmark I built a simple deterministic agent that wraps a Whisper ASR model.[^4] The agent transcribes the audio, runs a deliberately simple parser over the transcript, and decides whether to escalate based on Whisper's confidence. My agent's austere escalation policy looks like this:

```python
# voice_agent_eval/whisper_agent.py — the policy gate

def decide(asr_text: str, confidence: float) -> Action:
    parsed = parse_id_shape(asr_text)   # uppercase, strip, normalize

    if parsed is None or confidence < ESCALATE_BELOW:
        return Escalate()
    if confidence < CONFIRM_BELOW:
        return AskForRepeat()
    return Commit(parsed)
```
### Simplifying Assumptions Focus The Benchmark
What we're doing here is taking a surgical approach to checking a single domain-specific high-stakes failure mode. By discarding extraneous details we're able to better isolate what we're interested in.

Some of the complexities this setup removes:

- Both the benchmark's interlocutor and the agent I built to exercise the benchmark forego an actual LLM for the 'brain' of the agent. They're fully deterministic. Only the ASR model introduces uncertainty.
- We also forego any sequence of realistic conversational turns preceding the ID transcription challenge.
- The benchmark provides the agent very limited options: submit a transcript capture, escalate to a human, or ask to repeat the audio file.
- Importantly, the setup completely ignores many of the essential challenges of the voice agent domain: turn-taking, barge-ins, end-pointing, latency, etc. In fact, the benchmark is *not* even real-time. It's turn-based, with an optional HTTP-based FastAPI server. These real-time issues are not germane to the question our benchmark is asking.

It's also worth pointing out that the purpose of this benchmark is not to assess frontier capabilities, but rather to catch regressions. The current generation of agents can easily perform well on it already.

## What happened when I upgraded the model

I ran my agent on the benchmark with four different Whisper ASR model sizes. When I initially swapped them in without tuning the escalation policy threshold, accuracy increased monotonically while wrong-commit rates on the ~impossible 'severe' bucket tasks rose from 0% in tiny.en up to 64% in large-v3 😬.

### All the ways you could have missed this regression
The fix in this case turned out to be trivial but there are a lot of ways one could have missed this regression. Let's walk through the layers of how you might miss it in practice:

1. **Having an agent that has no way of knowing how confident it is in its transcription** (from my personal conversations, this seems to remain common as of this writing). In this case it's a moot point and you'll find out from your customers.
2. **Not specifically evaluating the failure mode you're interested in.** If it would be really bad for your voice agent to fail in production by mishearing certain info and not escalating, then your eval suite should test for that. It would be easy to gloss over in a broad end-to-end evaluation.
3. **Only looking at ID capture accuracy.** "Look! Accuracy increases with a bigger model." Ignoring the escalation behavior would miss the bug.
4. **Not including adversarial examples in the benchmark** If you only tested clean samples you also would miss it.
5. **Only looking at the aggregate wrong-commit rate.** Check out the amber line in the chart above. Overall the escalation behavior doesn't look so bad. But when you look specifically at the impossible cases, it's clear that the large model has *totally lost the plot*.
6. **Calibrating the model's escalation policy to this benchmark.** The distribution of severe audio samples is arbitrary and has no relation to any actual production situation. Below I do an F1 sweep to find the 'optimal' for each model. But the calibration settings only work for this distribution of fixtures.

## So what's actually going on with that threshold drift?

Recalibration works, but it's a band-aid — and notice what the band-aid quietly admits. The "right" threshold isn't a property of your task; it moved from 0.55 to 0.75 just because I swapped the model. That should bother you. A confidence of 0.7 evidently means something different coming out of large-v3 than coming out of tiny.en, and something different again depending on how bad the audio is. So before reaching for a better calibration trick, it's worth asking the more basic question: **what is this number actually measuring, and why does its meaning slide around?**

Start by being precise about where the number comes from. Whisper is an autoregressive encoder–decoder Transformer. The encoder turns the audio's log-mel spectrogram into a latent sequence; the decoder is a language model that emits text tokens one at a time, each conditioned on (a) the audio, via cross-attention, and (b) the tokens it has already emitted, via causal self-attention. At every step it produces a softmax over the entire ~51.9k-token BPE vocabulary and picks one. So it is genuinely next-token prediction — the only difference from a text-only LLM is the extra cross-attention into the audio.

And `exp(avg_logprob)` is *literally* the geometric mean of those per-step probabilities: `avg_logprob` is the mean of `log P(chosen token)` over the emitted tokens, so `exp(avg_logprob)` is the typical probability the decoder assigned to each word it committed to. Said another way, it is just **inverse perplexity** — perplexity is `exp(−avg_logprob)`, the standard measure of how surprised a language model is by a string — so the "confidence" your agent gates on is a *fluency* score, not a correctness one.[^6] High means it concentrated mass on what it emitted at each step (decisive); low means it spread its bets. It measures *decisiveness*, not fidelity to the audio — which is the whole story of this post.

You can watch the mechanism happen. Here is whisper-tiny.en decoding a spoken ID one token at a time — on clean audio (top) and on severe audio (bottom). Each panel is the model's probability distribution over the *next* token, top five candidates; the coloured bar is the token it actually committed to and fed back in for the following step.[^5]

On the clean line the distribution collapses onto one token at every step — `R → as → in → Romeo → …`, each a near-certain spike, which is exactly what a confidence near 1.0 looks like up close. On the severe line the same model is guessing: the very first token splits its mass across `You / Welcome / Oh / This / you`, nothing above a few percent. But watch what happens after it commits to "You" — the later steps sharpen right back up ("know what I'm going to…"), because once a fluent English sentence is underway it is easy to continue *as language*, regardless of whether it matches the audio. The confidence partially recovers — on a complete fabrication.

So the short answer is the one I keep circling back to: `exp(avg_logprob)` measures how sure the decoder was about the tokens it emitted, not whether those tokens are correct. To see how far those two things drift apart — and where — I had to stop trusting the benchmark's own scoring and measure "wrongness" more honestly.

### First, measuring "wrong" honestly

To ask whether confidence tracks correctness, I need the *other* axis: a single number per transcription for how wrong it actually was, that I can plot against the model's confidence. "Right vs. wrong" is too blunt — a transcript that drops one digit and one that invents a whole sentence are both "wrong," but they are not the same failure, and the question is precisely whether confidence falls as the wrongness grows. Character error rate gives that graded measure.

CER is just edit distance over the length of the reference:

```
CER = levenshtein(reference, transcript) / length(reference)
```

The [Levenshtein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levenshtein_distance) (edit) distance is the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions, or substitutions needed to turn one string into the other. Identical strings score 0; the more the transcript has to be rewritten to match what was said, the higher it climbs. Because the denominator is the *reference* length, a transcript longer than the truth — a fabricated sentence standing in for a short ID — can score well above 1.0.

The catch is that the *reference you compare against* changes the conclusion completely. My agent's committed value is the post-parser ID string, and if you score *that* against the canonical ID, a wild hallucination collapses to a near-empty string: a one-character slip and a twenty-word fabrication both land at a CER of about 1.0. The metric saturates exactly where the behavior gets interesting — it literally cannot tell you *how* wrong a confident hallucination is.

So instead I score the *raw transcript* against what was actually *spoken*, with no cap. The one bit of care: normalize away formatting that isn't a mishearing — lowercase, drop spaces and punctuation, treat "five" and "5" as identical — while keeping the actual words, so a genuine acoustic slip like hearing "tango" as "mango" still counts. A correct hearing then scores ≈0 however it was spelled out, and a hallucination is free to be as wrong as it really is.

A worked example. The rep says *"S as in sierra, X-ray, 1, 1, 8, 5"* and Whisper returns *"S is in Sierra, X-ray, 118.5."* Normalized, those become

```
ref: sasinsierraxray1185   (what was spoken)
hyp: sisinsierraxray1185   (what Whisper heard)
```

— one substitution (the "a" of "as" became the "i" of "is") over a 19-character reference, so CER = 1/19 ≈ **0.05**: a near-perfect hearing, and you can see exactly why. Now contrast a severe-audio hallucination — the rep spells out a short ID and Whisper returns *"Hello, welcome to this channel. Real fast, real fast…"* — which shares almost nothing with what was said, so the edit distance is essentially the entire fabricated sentence and the CER lands several times higher (up to ≈4× across this benchmark).[^7]

Plot every transcription that way — raw, uncapped error against the model's confidence — and the picture stops looking like a calibration curve and starts looking like weather.

### Confidence is a cloud, not a line

If you pool every transcription together, confidence and error are negatively correlated at about ρ = −0.83 (−0.81 with the abstentions set aside), which sounds reassuringly strong. But that number is inflated by mixing conditions. It mostly reflects that clean audio lives in one corner (high confidence, low error) and severe audio in another (low confidence, high error). Pooling the two manufactures a correlation that exists *between* conditions more than *within* any one of them — and at inference time you are always inside a single condition, never the pool.

Look within a single bucket — the only view that matches what your agent faces in the moment — and the relationship loosens and turns uneven. Pooled by tier it runs from about −0.34 (light, severe) to −0.75 (heavy); slice it finer into individual model×bucket cells and the median is just −0.43, with cells scattering from −0.72 all the way to slightly *positive*. So confidence is a soft, unreliable hint about correctness, not a tight proxy — and the rest of this section is about the three different ways that hint deteriorates as the channel degrades.

### Three things break, all at once

**1. The number stops meaning the same thing.** This is what "threshold drift" really is. It is not that the best cutoff wanders randomly; it's that the entire mapping from confidence to correctness slides as the audio worsens. A confidence of 0.55 means "probably fine" on clean audio and "confident misfire" on severe audio. And you can't correct for the slide, because the variable you'd need to condition on — how degraded this particular call is — is precisely what the bad audio prevents you from knowing.

**2. Right and wrong stop being separable.** On clean audio, correct transcriptions cluster high and the rare wrong one sits lower, so a threshold can split them. As distortion rises the two distributions slide together until they overlap almost entirely. The sharpest way to say it: ask how well confidence can *rank* a correct transcription above a wrong one (its AUROC as a detector). On clean audio it's a decent classifier. By the severe bucket the question is undefined — there are *zero* correct transcriptions, from any model, for confidence to surface. The signal you'd threshold on has nothing left to find.

**3. The model stops admitting defeat.** This is the one that scales with model size, and it's the most consequential. Abstention — returning an empty transcript — is the single fail-safe behavior available on unrecoverable audio. On severe audio, whisper-tiny abstains **58%** of the time; it heard noise and said nothing. Whisper-large abstains **0%** of the time. The biggest model has traded the one move that fails safe for a confident wrong answer, every single time.

You can read all three breakdowns off one operational number. Take a fixed gate — accept any transcript with confidence ≥ 0.55 — and ask how often what it accepts is actually correct. Clean: 90%. Moderate: 79%. Heavy: 62%. Severe: **0%**. The same gate that's a reliable filter on a good line is a rubber stamp on a bad one.

This is also the mechanism behind the regression, in miniature. On the severe bucket, the larger model doesn't just get more accurate on recoverable audio — it pushes *unrecoverable* audio into the region where a candidate exists and the confidence clears the bar:

| Severe bucket | tiny.en | small.en | medium.en | large-v3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Parser produced candidate on first turn | 7/25 | 18/25 | 17/25 | 22/25 |
| First-turn confidence ≥ 0.55 | 0/25 | 2/25 | 4/25 | 12/25 |
| Final wrong commit | 0/25 | 4/25 | 6/25 | 16/25 |

### The whisper-large paradox

Here's a subtlety worth slowing down for, because it looks like a contradiction. Whisper-large is the *most confidently wrong* model on severe audio. And yet, within its own outputs, its confidence is the *best-ordered* of any model: among the things it transcribes, the ones it's more confident about really are less wrong (its within-bucket ρ on severe is −0.54, the strongest of the four). How can it be both the worst-calibrated and the best-ranked?

Because those are two different properties. One is the *offset* — where the whole cloud of confidence sits. The other is the *slope* — whether, inside that cloud, more confidence means less error. A line can sit high and still slope down. Whisper-large does both: it shifts the entire cloud upward (more confident across the board — the headline) *and* tilts it so its internal ranking sharpens.

The catch is that the better ranking is operationally useless, because the whole range has floated above any sane cutoff. Whisper-large's *single most confident* transcription on the severe bucket — the one at the very top of its own scale, at 0.77 — is still a wrong ID. Its confidence does sag on its very worst hallucinations, but every output, top to bottom, sits above the line a clean-tuned threshold would draw. Better relative ranking, worse absolute calibration. The ranking gain buys you nothing when the model saves its highest confidence for fabrications.

One thing the per-model numbers make precise: that upward shift is not uniform. Whisper-large is far more confident than tiny where it has real signal — a median **0.91** on clean audio and **0.82** on its correct hearings, against tiny's 0.67 and 0.63 — but only marginally more confident on severe audio (0.53 vs 0.47). The cloud floated up mostly because the *correct* hearings climbed, and that is what drags the operating threshold up with it (0.55 → 0.75). On the fabrications themselves, large is barely more sure than the small models. So "bigger model, more confident wrong answers" is, more precisely, "bigger model *stops abstaining*, against a confidence scale that floated up on everything it gets right" — not the model believing its hallucinations more loudly.

### What "confidently wrong" actually is

It's tempting to picture the confident errors as the model going rogue and reciting a fluent fabrication. Click the confident-but-wrong dots in the plot above and listen, though, and you mostly hear something more mundane and more alarming: the model confidently *reading back an ID* — just the wrong one. Whisper-large, sure at 0.71, hears `O as in Oscar, 2, 6, 0, F as in Foxtrot, 0, 0` through the noise and returns a crisp, ID-shaped string with letters that were never spoken.

The wrong transcriptions split into two populations that sit at different confidence levels:

- **Garbled read-backs** — still letters and digits, just the wrong ones. Fewer of them (48 of 176 wrong transcripts), but they're the *confident* errors: median confidence **0.56**.
- **Fluent fabrications** — the off-topic "thank you for watching, see you next time" kind everyone pictures. More numerous (128 of 176), but the *least* confident: median **0.44**.

So the dots that clear a confidence gate are overwhelmingly read-backs, not fabrications — and that's the operationally dangerous half. A fabrication doesn't parse as an ID and gets rejected; a confident wrong read-back parses cleanly and commits. The failure that survives to production isn't the model inventing prose where it should have stayed silent — it's the model confidently mis-resolving the identifier it was actually handed.

### Why fluency reads as confidence

Step back and the mechanism is almost obvious. `exp(avg_logprob)` rewards fluent, self-consistent token sequences — that's all a language model's probability really tracks. A bigger model has a stronger language prior, so when the acoustic evidence turns to mush, it has more to fall back on. It doesn't stall; it generates a clean, well-formed, ID-shaped string of digits and letters that it is genuinely "sure" of *as a piece of language* — even though that string has nothing to do with what the caller said.

The confidence isn't a bug in the narrow sense. It's real. It's just confidence in a plausible, wrong ID. The smaller model, with a weaker prior, is more likely to throw up its hands and emit nothing — which is exactly the behavior you wanted. Scaling the model up makes it a better *language* model on degraded input *and strips its instinct to abstain* — and it's the second of those that turns it into a more dangerous *transcriber*.

And the failure mode generalizes. Across the severe and heavy buckets combined, roughly **one in five** transcriptions is *confidently very wrong* — it clears a 0.5 confidence bar while the raw transcript is more wrong than right. Those are precisely the cases a confidence guard exists to catch, and a confidence guard is structurally blind to them. (Not every hallucination is dangerous, to be clear — plenty are low-confidence and get escalated, which is the system working. The dangerous ones are the confident, ID-shaped read-backs — a plausible *wrong* identifier, not lurid prose — and bigger models make more of them.)

## What would be a better way than threshold recalibration?

Threshold recalibration is the first fix, not the final design. It works in this benchmark because it changes the policy boundary: more questionable transcripts get confirmed instead of immediately committed. That's useful, but it is still using a signal that measures token-certainty as if it measured correctness — and we've just seen that signal degrade in three different directions on the audio you care most about.

A production agent should combine several signals instead of leaning on the one:

1. **Per-model, per-condition calibration.** If the ASR model changes, recompute thresholds against a held-out set that includes cases where the right answer is escalation. Temperature scaling or isotonic regression can turn a raw score into a better-calibrated probability — but the calibration has to be against the task outcome you care about, and it can't fully rescue you when the underlying signal has no separating power left (as on severe audio).
2. **Validate against the world, not the model.** Whisper's confidence tells you how sure it was about its tokens; it cannot tell you the ID is real. Check the captured value against the things you actually know — format and checksum rules, whether it exists in your database. An out-of-band check catches the fluent fabrications that confidence waves through. `KLANESTGOMESTREESTREES...` should never reach the commit stage regardless of how confident the decoder was.
3. **Cross-attempt agreement.** When two attempts produce different candidate IDs, that disagreement is itself a signal — often a better one than either transcript's confidence. A production system should be more willing to escalate when the evidence changes across repeats.
4. **Treat silence as signal, and match the model to the channel.** A model that returns nothing on bad audio is doing you a favor. If you know you're on a degraded channel, a bigger ASR is not automatically the safer choice — it's the one most likely to replace "I didn't catch that" with a confident wrong answer.
5. **Cost-aware policy.** The optimal decision is not "maximize F1." A wrong committed ID can cause downstream harm; an extra human review is an operational cost. The threshold should reflect that asymmetry, not a symmetric accuracy metric.

The broader point is that a voice agent should not ask, "Was the ASR confident?" It should ask, "Given the audio, transcript, task context, and downstream cost, is there enough evidence to commit this field?" The benchmark is useful because it makes that question measurable — and because it shows, concretely, how the easy answer fails.

## Reflections

> If you don't know where you are going, you might wind up someplace else.

— Yogi Berra

Whether in life or in evals, it pays to be clear on what you want.

When I built the benchmark, I didn't predict the model-swap miscalibration bug. Indeed if I were building a real agent, the added complexity would surely create opportunities for numerous other bugs. The lesson here is certainly *not* "remember to calibrate parameters when you swap models", nor is it even, "design systems that calibrate themselves so you can set it and forget it". The lesson is that we often can't predict the problems that will arise. We should therefore get specific about what we care about, and how we'll know whether our agent is sufficiently aligned in the dimensions we care about (ideally) before we release it into the wild.[^3]

By being clear on the product-level implications of different failure modes, we can specify what it means for our agent to be shippable. In the case of my benchmark, one presumably really cares not just about transcription accuracy but also about generally preventing wrong transcription. It's good to be clear about that from the start so whatever unexpected bugs arise will be surfaced by your evals in the dimensions you care about.

Finally, as many wise people have said: "As George Box once said, 'all models are wrong, but some are useful.'" By understanding how our proxy metric diverges from reality, we stand a better chance at identifying root causes of our agents' aberrant behavior. By reasoning through why the map is different from the territory we can make map-v2 more robust. Otherwise we resort to band-aid solutions.

In conclusion then, if you want your agent to know when it mishears, make sure you reason through *how* the agent 'knows'. Don't just be like, "was the ASR confident?" Given the audio, transcript, task context, and downstream cost, is there enough evidence to commit this field? And if nothing else, make sure your evals measure what you care about, so the bugs you didn't predict still pop up somewhere you're looking.
