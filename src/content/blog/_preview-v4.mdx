*Building a voice-agent benchmark for degraded audio scenarios.*

<Callout type="info">
**TL;DR**

If your voice agent needs to capture a critical piece of info like an ID, a medication, or a street address, how does it know if it heard correctly? Real-world audio channels are often distorted, and when failure is unacceptable, you need something more robust than just asking for confirmation.

The natural answer is to lean on the ASR's confidence number. The trouble is that this number is task-blind. It can tell you how confidently the ASR decoded a transcript, but not whether the transcript is the right answer to the question your agent asked.

I built a targeted benchmark to test this specific critical failure mode for voice agents: reliable ID capture given degraded audio quality. When I swapped in stronger Whisper models, capture accuracy improved — but on deliberately-unrecoverable audio, wrong commits rose sharply unless I recalibrated the downstream policy.
</Callout>

## The question

Voice agents are being deployed in increasingly high-stakes scenarios. We're still in the early phases of the rollout. Today, a critical failure often means lost revenues. But a future where we rely on voice agents for safety-critical roles means that effective evaluation becomes essential for health and safety.

Imagine a medical scribe agent capturing the wrong medication name, an emergency dispatch agent capturing the wrong street address, or an air traffic control agent hearing the wrong flight number. These errors could result in harm to humans.

The examples above are all instances of the critical failure mode of mishearing key information. The thing the agent is supposed to do when it can't reliably hear is *escalate to a human*. So the practical question, for anyone building one of these agents, is: how does it know?

## The obvious answer, and why it isn't enough

Most ASR models expose a confidence number. For Whisper, the natural one is `exp(avg_logprob)` — the geometric mean of the per-token probabilities assigned to the transcript Whisper decoded.[^2] You can wire that into an escalation policy with three lines: above some threshold, commit; below another, escalate; in between, ask the rep to repeat. That's what my agent does.

The catch is that this number is task-blind. It measures how confidently Whisper produced its chosen transcript, not whether that transcript is the right answer to the question the agent asked. It doesn't know the agent is trying to capture an ID. It doesn't know what ID format is expected. It doesn't know what a downstream parser will extract from the transcript.

That distinction matters most on degraded audio. A transcript can be fluent, repetitive, or internally self-consistent enough to receive a decent token-level score, while still being completely wrong for the task. If the parser can turn that transcript into some identifier-shaped string, and the confidence clears the commit threshold, the agent ships a wrong ID.

The surprising part in my benchmark was that this failure got worse as I upgraded Whisper. Larger models captured more recoverable IDs, as expected. But on the deliberately-unrecoverable audio, they also produced more wrong transcripts that crossed the parser + confidence gates. The result was a model upgrade that looked better on capture rate while becoming less safe on the cases where the right answer was "I can't hear this."

The rest of this post is about how I found that regression, what the data looks like, and what production systems should do besides trusting a raw ASR confidence threshold.

## The benchmark

<AgentFlowDemo />

The benchmark simulates the challenge of capturing essential info over degraded audio channels. I generated 100 test cases (200 audio fixtures total, two attempts per case) using the ElevenLabs TTS API. Each fixture is a short spoken identifier, e.g. *"S as in sierra, X-ray, one, one, eight, five"* (so the canonical ground truth here would be: `SX1185`). The cases span five buckets of increasingly degraded audio. Fixtures in the 'severe' bucket are designed to be unrecoverable.

| Bucket | n | Distortion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| clean | 15 | none |
| light | 15 | mild noise OR phone bandpass alone |
| moderate | 20 | phone bandpass + 10 dB SNR noise |
| heavy | 25 | phone bandpass + 5 dB SNR + light dropout |
| severe | 25 | phone bandpass + −12 dB SNR + 250 ms dropout @ 55% per-window |

The deterministic degradation pipeline simulates the sort of distortion artifacts that you hear on a phone call (you might say it's a bit phone-y[^1]): a 300–3400 Hz bandpass (the standard phone band), a μ-law codec roundtrip (the standard phone codec), additive white noise at a configurable SNR, and packet dropout where 250 ms windows are silenced at a configurable probability. The severe bucket layers all four and pushes things beyond human-level recoverability. Also, the catalog is seeded, so rerunning the generator produces byte-identical fixtures.

For each case, the agent being tested receives the audio file and chooses one of three actions: commit a transcription, ask the rep to repeat (one retry allowed), or escalate to a human. To recap, on the cases where the audio is actually unintelligible, the right behavior is to escalate. If your agent is recording something like a medication, an emergency address, or a patient identifier, you want it to fail loudly. The benchmark then grades the agent on % accuracy as well as escalation precision/recall (i.e. to what extent did it escalate on impossible cases but not on tractable cases?).

To exercise the benchmark I built a simple deterministic agent that wraps a Whisper ASR model.[^4] The agent transcribes the audio, runs a deliberately simple parser over the transcript, and decides whether to escalate based on Whisper's confidence. My agent's austere escalation policy looks like this:

```python
# voice_agent_eval/whisper_agent.py — the policy gate

def decide(asr_text: str, confidence: float) -> Action:
    parsed = parse_id_shape(asr_text)   # uppercase, strip, normalize

    if parsed is None or confidence < ESCALATE_BELOW:
        return Escalate()
    if confidence < CONFIRM_BELOW:
        return AskForRepeat()
    return Commit(parsed)
```

What we're doing here is taking a surgical approach to checking a single domain-specific high-stakes failure mode. By discarding extraneous details we're able to better isolate what we're interested in. The toy part is the implementation; the production pattern is real. Every voice agent that captures high-impact fields needs some policy for when the audio evidence is strong enough to commit and when it should ask again or escalate.

Some of the complexities this setup removes:

- Both the benchmark's interlocutor and the agent I built to exercise the benchmark forego an actual LLM for the 'brain' of the agent. They're fully deterministic. Only the ASR model introduces uncertainty.
- We also forego any sequence of realistic conversational turns preceding the ID transcription challenge.
- The benchmark provides the agent very limited options: submit a transcript capture, escalate to a human, or ask to repeat the audio file.
- Importantly, the setup completely ignores many of the essential challenges of the voice agent domain: turn-taking, barge-ins, end-pointing, latency, etc. In fact, the benchmark is *not* even real-time. It's turn-based, with an optional HTTP-based FastAPI server. These real-time issues are not germane to the question our benchmark is asking.

It's also worth pointing out that the purpose of this benchmark is not to assess frontier capabilities, but rather to catch regressions. The current generation of agents can easily perform well on it already.

## What happened when I upgraded the model

I ran my agent on the benchmark with four different Whisper ASR model sizes. When I initially swapped them in without tuning the escalation policy threshold, accuracy increased monotonically while wrong-commit rates on the ~impossible 'severe' bucket tasks rose from 0% in tiny.en up to 64% in large-v3 😬.

This is the kind of regression that's easy to miss. Let's walk through the layers of how you might miss it in practice:

1. **Having an agent that has no way of knowing how confident it is in its transcription** (from my personal conversations, this seems to remain common as of this writing). In this case it's a moot point and you'll find out from your customers.
2. **Not specifically evaluating the failure mode you're interested in.** If it would be really bad for your voice agent to fail in production by mishearing certain info and not escalating, then your eval suite should test for that. It would be easy to gloss over in a broad end-to-end evaluation.
3. **Only looking at ID capture accuracy.** "Look! Accuracy increases with a bigger model." Ignoring the escalation behavior would miss the bug.
4. **Not including adversarial examples in the benchmark** (assuming that sort of thing might show up in your production environment).
5. **Only looking at the aggregate wrong-commit rate.** Check out the amber line in the chart above. Overall the escalation behavior doesn't look so bad. But when you look specifically at the impossible cases, it's clear that the large model has *totally lost the plot*.
6. **Calibrating the model's escalation policy to this benchmark.** The distribution of severe audio samples is arbitrary and has no relation to any actual production situation. Below I do an F1 sweep to find the 'optimal' for each model. But the calibration settings only work for this distribution of fixtures.

## The band-aid: recalibrate per model

Once I caught the bug I found new thresholds for each model by doing a threshold sweep and finding the optimal F1 score for each model (since presumably we want a good balance of recall and precision). Remember that what we mean here by a true positive is a genuinely impossible case, a true negative is a genuinely tractable case, a false positive is a tractable case you thought was impossible, and a false negative is an impossible case you thought was tractable. That produced the following nice-looking chart (remember that you'd want to grab data from your actual domain to see how frequently adversarial cases occur in practice):

## So what's actually going on with that threshold drift?

The tempting story is: "bigger Whisper hallucinated more confidently." That's too simple. Hallucinated transcripts can be low-confidence, and some failures in this benchmark are not ASR failures at all. The mechanism is a stack interaction:

```text
audio -> ASR transcript + exp(avg_logprob)
      -> parser extracts candidate ID
      -> policy commits / confirms / repeats / escalates
```

`exp(avg_logprob)` only scores the first line. It measures the decoder's confidence in the tokens it chose. It does not know whether those tokens are a plausible ID, whether the parser will extract a value from them, or whether a wrong value is more expensive than an extra escalation.

On the severe bucket, the dangerous region grew with model size. These are the 25 cases designed to be unrecoverable; no model captured the canonical ID on any of them. The right behavior is to escalate.

| Severe bucket | tiny.en | small.en | medium.en | large-v3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Parser produced candidate on first turn | 7/25 | 18/25 | 17/25 | 22/25 |
| First-turn confidence ≥ 0.55 | 0/25 | 2/25 | 4/25 | 12/25 |
| Final wrong commit | 0/25 | 4/25 | 6/25 | 16/25 |

That table is the mechanism in miniature. The larger models do not merely become more accurate on the recoverable audio. They also move more unrecoverable audio into the "candidate exists + confidence clears threshold" region, where this toy agent commits instead of escalating.

### Not every hallucination is dangerous

A hallucinated sentence is not, by itself, the bug. For example, in `sev_096`, small.en heard:

```text
He was very popular, he had a good heart, he was one of six.
```

The confidence was only 0.39, so the agent escalated. This is the system working as designed. The transcript is nonsense for the task, but it never crosses the confidence gate.

### The dangerous cases cross both gates

`sev_098` is a cleaner example of the failure. The canonical ID is `117PS896`, but the audio is too degraded to recover. tiny.en fails loudly; the larger models produce digit-like transcripts that clear the commit threshold.

Notice the precise failure condition. It is not "ASR hallucinated." It is:

```text
bad audio
+ ASR hallucination
+ parser extracts a candidate
+ exp(avg_logprob) clears the commit threshold
+ no semantic or schema validator blocks it
= wrong commit
```

This also explains why recalibration helps. It moves more borderline transcripts into the confirmation path. In `sev_092`, large-v3 eventually hears `3-8-8-0-7-4-1-6` at confidence 0.70 and commits `38807416` under the default threshold. With the recalibrated threshold, the agent confirms first, gets "No, that does not match," and escalates.

### Some failures are validation failures

Other examples show why threshold recalibration is not the whole answer. In `sev_076`, large-v3 hears:

```text
K-L-A-N-E-S-T-G-O-M-E-S-T-R-E-E-S-T-R-E-E-S-T-R-E-E-S-T-R-E-E-S-T-R-E-E-S
```

The confidence is 0.62, which is high enough to clear the default commit threshold. But the parsed value `KLANESTGOMESTREESTREESTREESTREESTREES` is not a plausible member ID. A production system should reject it with a schema or length check before confidence thresholding even matters.

`sev_099` is similar. The canonical claim ID is `CL9235-57560`, but large-v3 hears:

```text
Human heart, all over me, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 6, 0.
```

The parser extracts `ME123456560` and the confidence is 0.63. A task-aware system cannot recover the real claim ID from that transcript, but it can recognize that the transcript is not good evidence for a claim ID and escalate.

### Some failures are extraction failures

There is also the opposite case: the transcript contains recoverable information, but my toy parser mishandles it. In `cle_005`, every model hears some variant of:

```text
U.S. in Uniform 59222.
```

The canonical ID is `U59222`, but the parser outputs `USU59222`. A task-aware extractor — even a small LLM with the instruction "extract the ID the rep is spelling" — could reasonably infer that "U.S. in Uniform" is an ASR rendering of "U as in Uniform." That case is not an ASR calibration failure. It is an extraction failure.

This is why I do not want to oversell the empirical labels as pure audio truth. The cleanest production-harm metric here is label-free: did the agent commit a value that differed from the canonical? The mechanism section is about the failures where bad audio, task-blind confidence, permissive parsing, and a commit policy line up in the wrong direction.

## What would be a better way than threshold recalibration?

Threshold recalibration is the first fix, not the final design. It works in this benchmark because it changes the policy boundary: more questionable transcripts get confirmed instead of immediately committed. That's useful, but it is still using a task-blind signal as if it were a task-aware one.

A production agent should combine several signals:

1. **Per-model calibration.** If the ASR model changes, recompute the confidence thresholds against a held-out set that includes cases where the right answer is escalation. Temperature scaling or isotonic regression can turn a raw score into a better-calibrated probability, but the calibration has to be against the task outcome you care about.
2. **Task-aware extraction.** The transcript is not the final answer. An extraction layer should use the fact that the agent asked for a member ID, claim ID, medication, address, or dollar amount. This can be a stricter deterministic parser, a grammar, or an LLM constrained to return `{value, uncertainty, reason}`.
3. **Schema and plausibility validation.** If the expected member ID is short and alphanumeric, `KLANESTGOMESTREESTREESTREESTREESTREES` should not reach the commit stage. If the transcript sounds like a random sentence, the system should treat that as evidence of uncertainty, not as a candidate answer.
4. **Cross-attempt agreement.** When two attempts produce different candidate IDs, that disagreement is itself a signal. A production system should be more willing to escalate when the evidence changes across repeats.
5. **Cost-aware policy.** The optimal decision is not just "maximize F1." A wrong committed ID can cause downstream harm, while an extra human review is an operational cost. The threshold should reflect that asymmetry.

The broader point is that a voice agent should not ask, "Was the ASR confident?" It should ask, "Given the audio, transcript, task context, parser output, and downstream cost, is there enough evidence to commit this field?" The benchmark is useful because it makes that question measurable.

## Reflections

> If you don't know where you are going, you might wind up someplace else.

— Yogi Berra

Whether in life or in evals, it pays to be clear on what you want.

When I built the benchmark, I didn't predict the model-swap miscalibration bug. Indeed if I were building a real agent, the added complexity would surely create opportunities for numerous other bugs. The lesson here is certainly *not* "remember to calibrate parameters when you swap models", nor is it even, "design systems that calibrate themselves so you can set it and forget it". The lesson is that we often can't predict the problems that will arise. We should therefore get specific about what we care about, and how we'll know whether our agent is sufficiently aligned in the dimensions we care about (ideally) before we release it into the wild.[^3]

By being clear on the product-level implications of different failure modes, we can specify what it means for our agent to be shippable. In the case of my benchmark, one presumably really cares not just about transcription accuracy but also about generally preventing wrong transcription. It's good to be clear about that from the start so whatever unexpected bugs arise will be surfaced by your evals in the dimensions you care about.


Second, as per the oft-quoted George Box, "all models are wrong but some are useful." By understanding how our proxy metric diverges from reality, we can get a better sense of where the system needs another layer of evidence.

A multimodal model might condition on more conversational context than this toy setup, but its downstream policy still needs some way to decide whether the evidence is strong enough to commit a high-impact field.
